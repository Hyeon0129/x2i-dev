## Part 2: Reality hit hard

I knew I had to find work. The problem was obvious: zero Japanese ability, zero work experience in Japan, zero way to pass a normal job interview. I did what I could. Circulated resumes, walked around asking everywhere, posted in online communities for Korean expats. Eventually one place took me. A dosirak shop inside a major subway station. Two days a week, 7 AM to 5 PM. Kitchen prep work. Most of the staff were ethnic Koreans from China, so at least I could understand what was being said.

The income was $435 a month. Rent alone ate most of it. After transportation, utilities, and food, the math didn't remotely work. But I couldn't speak Japanese. What else was I going to get? There was nothing. So I took it, and I kept looking.

2019 November, my wife's belly had grown noticeably. The due date was early 2020 February. I ran the numbers. If I couldn't leave Japan to be there, I needed to be earning. If I left, I'd lose the job and whatever foothold I had. The conclusion was ugly but clear: I wouldn't be there when my son was born.

December came. $1,090 left. Someone could reasonably ask: why not just go back to Korea? Crash with family, work manual labor, get back on your feet. It's the rational move. But I was 22 years old and whatever part of my brain handles humility wasn't functioning at the time. What I thought instead was: *I'd rather starve to death here than ask anyone for help. I'll survive by any means necessary.* I stayed.

Before she gave birth, I had to see her. I flew to the Philippines in December. Her belly was huge. We went to an OB-GYN and I heard the heartbeat for the first time. That small, fast sound from inside her. I remember standing there thinking: there is absolutely no version of this story where I quit.

Two weeks later I flew back to Japan. **$580 in the account.**

January 14, 2020. Found a new part-time job, finally. A Korean-owned restaurant. The posted rate was around $9 an hour but I had no Japanese and couldn't cook, so we agreed on about $7. I told myself it was a start.

Two weeks later, I was fired. The reason: I couldn't speak Japanese and couldn't cook. Which they had known from the start, and which was exactly why I was being paid less. Didn't matter. Out.

The date was January 28.
 
### Also January 28: my son was born.

I found out walking home in the rain. Middle of January, Tokyo winter, no jacket, slippers on my feet because I had no money for shoes. Fired in the early morning hours and walking back in the dark. My phone buzzed. A photo from my wife.
 
That was the first time I saw my son.

Money left: $240. I sent $232 of it to my wife for medicine and initial costs. The hospital still needed another $220 that I didn't have. I told her: give me two weeks, I'll get it to you somehow.

At home: ten packs of instant ramen, a little rice, a few bottles of water. $7 to my name. And a subway pass with 12 days left. That was the entire situation.

The pass mattered more than it sounds. Without it I couldn't job-hunt, couldn't get to interviews, couldn't get to school. The nearest alternative on foot was 30km away. 12 days. That was my window.

From that night I searched until 2 AM every day. Called back every number the next morning, scheduled every interview I could. One week in, I found it. A sauna in Roppongi. 3 PM to 11 PM, days negotiable. About $9 an hour, plus commuting allowance up to $145 a month. If I could get six days a week, the math worked out to roughly $1,390 a month. I knew the moment I saw it: this was the one. Miss this and I'm stealing ramen from convenience stores.

I went to the interview and said exactly what I meant.
 
***"Please hire me. I'll do everything. My Japanese isn't good but I'll learn fast. You can pay me less. Please."***

February 12, 2020. First day at the sauna. The same day I got paid for the two weeks I'd worked at the restaurant before getting fired. I used that money to cover my wife's remaining hospital bill. She took our son and went home.

The sauna was in Roppongi. The clientele was almost entirely yakuza. It was the only sauna in the area that allowed tattoos, so that made a certain kind of sense. I didn't speak Japanese. I got yelled at constantly. I couldn't tell the actual words from the tone, but the tone was clear enough.
 
I didn't care. I was working.

Even a mountain of laundry to deal with was something to be glad about.

For the first time since July, I felt like things had shifted. I could send money to my wife. I could eat. I could stay in school. I thought: *I've turned this around.*

That feeling lasted about two months.
