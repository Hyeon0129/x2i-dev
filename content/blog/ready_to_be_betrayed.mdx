There's a line I heard relentlessly growing up. Not just me, most people who came of age hearing this kind of thing absorbed some version of it. *Effort never betrays you.* I believed it without question. I carried it into adulthood like scripture. When things got genuinely hard, that belief was what I pressed against. It was my value system and my conviction both. Someday, I told myself, this will pass. The effort will accumulate and unlock something better. The life I'd imagined.

**[I've mentioned this elsewhere on this blog](/blog/life/becoming_dad_part4)**, but there was a period when I pushed past every limit I had, physical and mental both. Already beyond my threshold, still going, with nothing in my head except *effort is the only answer.* So what happened? I collapsed. The body broke. The psychological damage was real. It was the event that warped the belief itself.

Looking back now, the effort I put in during that time exceeded anything I would consider reasonable even by today's standards. And it still didn't work. **Effort betrayed me.** It didn't bring a better life. It brought damage. I didn't understand the reason at the time, but it's obvious now. There were better paths available. But effort, as a motivating force, had plugged my ears. I stopped taking in information from the outside. The only thing I could see was the one path in front of me, and the instruction to stay on it.

I could sense I was going in the wrong direction, dimly. A smaller effort toward a better target would have served me more. But switching paths felt, at the time, like quitting, like I was stopping because it was hard, not because something else made more sense. So I kept going.

Some degree of effort is obviously required to get what you want. That's not the point. The point is that effort alone does not solve everything. Effort is one variable. **The world is not a single-variable equation.**

During COVID, my income dropped to a quarter. There were months with no floor under me. Was that my fault? No. Forces I couldn't control moved through the economy and took things with them.

I don't regret the effort itself. I regret the tunnel vision, the belief that there was only one path, and that staying on it was the same as being right. If I were facing the same situation today, I would try something entirely different. Since then, I've held a different principle: effort, yes, but effort isn't everything. And I've come to accept that some amount of luck is also in the equation.

Looking back, the only time effort reliably produced outcomes was school. There's a phrase older people say, *you'll miss your school days*, and I think I understand part of it now. Not just the energy. Not just the novelty of being young. But this: nothing in life tracks effort as cleanly as studying. Study more, score higher. Study more, get into a better school. It's almost mechanical. But the world outside school doesn't work that way. Even studying, I now understand, involved talent and luck more than I admitted at the time. The real world has no such clean mapping. Many variables interact. Results emerge from conditions you didn't set.

Jensen Huang worked hard. Elon Musk worked hard. But for that effort to become what it became, conditions outside their control also had to align, the era they were born into, their health, the people they happened to meet. None of those are things effort can arrange.

COVID was that force for me. Now, AI is playing that role for many people. Law firms have stopped hiring junior associates. The same is happening in software. Not because those people didn't try. A current moving through the economy shifted the structure, and effort had nothing to do with it. AI has barely been around ten years, and the displacement is already visible across multiple industries.

People feel the sting of this. They didn't get the job they wanted. They tried and still came up short. Some say their life is over because of a failed application to one company. One hiring decision. The world doesn't work that cleanly. Enormous numbers of people form networks. A single small thing creates chains of cause and effect that change everything. Plans break. A hire that seemed certain falls through for a reason no one can name.

I am not saying stop trying. Effort is still necessary. What I'm saying is: effort is not the only thing. And when you try, keep one eye on the possibility that it won't work, not as a reason to stop, but as a way to survive the disappointment when it arrives. Try. But **always be ready for effort to betray you.** Don't let effort become the thing that blinds you, that closes your eyes and ears to other paths. That's what I want to say.

**Quitting is also a strategy.** Not quitting without having tried, but setting a threshold before you start, and being willing to walk away when you cross it. It's not easy. But it's better to stop before the bigger disappointment arrives. We owe it to ourselves.

When I was young, one path dominated everyone's thinking: good high school, good university, good company, save up for a house, raise children, work until retirement. That path still lives in people's heads. But it no longer maps to the world. There are too many new things now, too many different ways. The old route doesn't work the way it used to.

To whoever is reading this: I hope you don't carry the sting of effort's betrayal as something that was your fault. It may not have been. Try, but don't turn it into self-punishment. Always be ready for it not to work. Life doesn't go according to plan, that's what makes it life. If it followed a script the way a game does, it would be dull. Everything is part of it. Take it in.

**This is not self-rationalization. It is a factual solution.**
